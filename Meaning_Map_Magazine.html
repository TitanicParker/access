The big idea

02

A sentence is more than a row of words.

It guides attention, completes a movement, and leaves something behind.

Traditional grammar tells us what words are called. The Meaning Map asks a different question: what is the sentence doing as we read it?

Every sentence opens a small space of uncertainty - then closes it in a particular way.

Take a very simple sentence: She kept her house neat. At first, kept could mean several things. The final word, neat, settles the meaning. The sentence has moved toward one condition and left that condition in place.

1. The job

What is the sentence doing?

Pointing, connecting, building, turning or settling.

2. The path

Where does attention move?

Toward one focus, or across several steps.

3. The after-effect

What remains when it ends?

A state, an active effect, or two things held together.

Those three questions create a compact reading. You do not need technical training to use it. Read the sentence naturally, notice where it lands, and name the shape of the movement.

What this is

A practical lens for noticing how meaning unfolds in real time - useful for reading, writing, editing and teaching.

What this is not

A replacement for grammar. It sits beside grammar and focuses on the experience of the whole sentence.

The system at a glance

03

Three questions. Ten plain-language choices.

The original system terms are shown in italics so the deeper framework remains visible.

The job

What is it doing?

  • Point Distinction
    Introduces or identifies one thing.
  • Connect Relation
    Links a person, action, object or event.
  • Build Expansion
    Adds pieces to make a larger picture.
  • Turn Correction
    Changes the direction or expected outcome.
  • Settle Rest
    Establishes a habit, condition or repeated pattern.
The path

Where does it move?

  • Toward
    Narrows toward one main point.
  • Across
    Moves through a list, sequence or contrast.
The after-effect

What remains?

  • State Is
    Leaves a fact, identity or completed situation.
  • Action Does
    Leaves an effect still operating in the meaning.
  • Held With
    Keeps two truths present together.
A full reading can be written as a three-part stamp: job + path + after-effect.

Example: She swept the floor each morning. The sentence connects an action to an object, narrows toward the floor, and leaves a completed fact.

See it in the story

04

First, the world is introduced and made familiar.

The story begins by pointing, connecting and settling into a routine.

Example 1

Once upon a time, there was a little red hen...

The opening creates an empty story-space, then brings one clear character into focus. Once hen arrives, she stands as the person the story is about.

When the story turns

05

Contrast changes the direction of meaning.

A single word - often but - can overturn what seemed likely to happen.

Example 4

She turned quickly, but the fox sprang forward and caught her.

The hen seems to recover. Then but overturns that direction: the fox acts, and his action decides the outcome.

The larger pattern

06

A whole story can be seen as a sequence of sentence moves.

The system reveals not only what happens, but how the telling changes shape.

How the story changes shape
Story phase A line from the story The movement
ArrivalThere was a little red hen...Point → Toward → State
RoutineShe kept her house neat.Settle → Toward → Held
ThreatThe sly fox crept from behind a bush.Connect → Toward → State
Reversal...but the fox sprang forward...Turn → Across → Action
EscapeShe slipped out... and hid behind a tree.Connect → Across → State
New habitShe listened carefully whenever the leaves rustled.Settle → Across → State
The plot moves from introduction, to routine, to danger, to reversal, to a wiser routine.

This is where the system becomes more than a label. Repeated sentence shapes create rhythm. Turns create surprise. Across-movements create momentum. Settling movements create the sense that a new normal has been reached.

Try it yourself

07

The one-minute Meaning Map test

Choose any sentence. Read it once for sense, then answer these three questions.

1

Name the job

Is it pointing, connecting, building, turning or settling?

2

Trace the path

Does attention narrow toward one point, or move across several parts?

3

Notice the after-effect

Are you left with a state, an active effect, or two truths held together?

Do not hunt for the correct label too quickly. First notice the movement you actually experience.
For writers

Use the map to vary rhythm. Too many Connect + Toward sentences may feel flat; a Build or Turn can change the pace.

For readers

Use it to explain why a sentence feels calm, surprising, cumulative, balanced or final.

The Meaning Map begins with a simple promise: sentences are not static containers. They unfold. Once you learn to see that movement, familiar writing becomes newly visible.
